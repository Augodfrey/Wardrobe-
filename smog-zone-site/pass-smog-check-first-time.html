No Tricks, Just Good Preparation

No secret trick will make a car with a real emissions problem pass. But plenty of vehicles fail for reasons that are easy to avoid, like a code cleared the day before or a battery swapped last week.

California's Bureau of Automotive Repair (BAR) recommends that drivers check a few key things before the inspection: the check engine light, recent battery work, readiness monitors, and the vehicle's software. Here's how to give your car the best chance of passing on the first visit.

1

Start With the Check Engine Light

This matters more than anything else. If the check engine light is on because of an emissions-related fault, the vehicle will not pass a California smog check.

BAR's advice is simple: get the problem repaired before the test. Turning the light off on the way to the station is not a repair. It usually just creates a different problem (see tip 2).

2

Don't Clear Codes Right Before Your Test

Clearing trouble codes with a scan tool, or disconnecting the battery, can switch the check engine light off for a while. It also resets your readiness monitors.

Readiness monitors are self-tests your car's computer runs to confirm the emissions systems are working. They usually need to be complete for the vehicle to pass the OBD part of the smog check. Clear the codes the morning of the test and you'll likely just be sent home to drive and come back.

3

New or Disconnected Battery? Give It Time

This one surprises a lot of drivers. Your readiness monitors may have reset if your battery was recently:

  • Replaced
  • Disconnected
  • Fully drained
  • Jump-started after dying in a way that wiped the computer's memory

BAR suggests driving normally for about a week or two after battery work before getting your smog check. There's no set mileage, because each vehicle needs different conditions to finish its monitors, including speed, temperature, engine load and more.

Quick tipIf your registration renewal is coming up, avoid disconnecting the battery right before your smog check unless you really have to.
4

Confirm Your Readiness Monitors Are Complete

No check engine light doesn't always mean you're ready. Monitors can still be incomplete, especially after:

  • Clearing trouble codes
  • Replacing the battery
  • Disconnecting the battery
  • Recent emissions repairs

After repairs or battery resets, the monitors have to run again. If you own an OBD scan tool, check monitor status before the official test. If you're not sure, a quick status check can save you a second trip.

5

Don't Try to Outsmart the Computer

Old internet advice says to disconnect the battery right before smog to wipe the codes. On modern vehicles, that usually backfires.

For many 2010 and newer vehicles, California also checks for Permanent Diagnostic Trouble Codes. Permanent codes can't be erased with a scan tool or a battery disconnect. They only clear once the car's computer confirms the underlying problem is fixed. The real fix is to repair the actual problem.

6

Tuned Car? Make Sure the Software Is California-Legal

Performance tunes are a common cause of unexpected failures. BAR advises owners of vehicles with modified software to return to either:

  • The manufacturer's original (stock) software, or
  • A version approved by the California Air Resources Board (CARB)

Non-approved software can fail the inspection. This is especially worth checking on performance cars, diesel trucks, and anything with an aftermarket tuner.

7

Look for Obvious Emissions Equipment Problems

A California smog check can include a visual inspection of the emissions-control equipment that applies to your vehicle. Before your visit, look for things like:

  • A missing catalytic converter
  • Disconnected emissions hoses
  • Damaged or removed emissions parts
  • Non-approved aftermarket equipment
  • Loose or broken components

If you recently had a catalytic converter or another emissions part replaced, make sure the replacement is legal for your specific vehicle in California.

8

Make Sure the Vehicle Is Safe to Test

A smog technician can refuse to test a vehicle with an obvious safety hazard. For older vehicles that get a tailpipe test, inspectors are required to check for hazards before starting.

Before you come in, make sure there's nothing like a major fluid leak, unsafe tires, or another issue that would make running the vehicle during the inspection unsafe.

9

Drive Normally Before the Test

Vehicles that get a tailpipe emissions test need to be at normal operating temperature first. Bringing an older vehicle in after regular driving is better than arriving stone-cold after it's been sitting for days.

A normal 15 to 20 minute drive is plenty. There's no need to rev the engine, drive hard, or try to "heat up" the catalytic converter.

10

Avoid Last-Minute Repairs

A very common mistake is doing emissions work and driving straight to the smog station. If the repair cleared the codes, the readiness monitors haven't had time to run again. After an emissions repair, wait until:

  • The underlying problem is actually fixed
  • The check engine light stays off
  • The relevant monitors have completed
  • The vehicle has had enough normal driving time

Then come in for your official test.

11

Look Up Your Smog History

The Bureau of Automotive Repair offers an official online Vehicle Inspection History lookup. Search by VIN or license plate to see your previous smog inspections and their results.

It's especially handy if you just bought the vehicle or aren't sure when it was last tested.

Read Your DMV Notice for the Word "STAR"

Look closely at your registration renewal notice. If it says:

DMV notice"Smog Certification Required at a STAR Station"

then you must go to a STAR-certified station. A test at a non-STAR station won't meet the DMV requirement.

Smog "Tricks" That Won't Get You a Pass

The internet is full of smog hacks that sound convincing. None of these fix an emissions problem, so don't count on them to get a failing vehicle through:

Filling up with premium when your car takes regular
Disconnecting the battery
Clearing the check engine light
Pouring in random fuel additives
Driving extremely hard right before the test
Replacing parts without diagnosing the problem

A smog check looks at your actual emissions-control system and, on applicable vehicles, the data your car's computer reports. A well-maintained vehicle with no known emissions problems is the surest path to a pass.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should I drive my car before a smog check?

Yes, normal driving is ideal. Older vehicles that get a tailpipe emissions test need to be at normal operating temperature before testing, and a 15 to 20 minute drive takes care of that.

How long should I drive after replacing my battery?

BAR's general guidance is to drive for about a week or two after disconnecting or replacing the battery, so your readiness monitors have a chance to complete.

Can I clear my check engine light right before the test?

We don't recommend it. Clearing codes resets readiness monitors, and permanent diagnostic trouble codes on many newer vehicles can't simply be erased.

Will my car pass if there's no check engine light?

Not necessarily. The vehicle still has to meet the other visual, functional, OBD, or emissions requirements that apply to it.

Should I use a fuel additive before my smog check?

No additive guarantees a passing California smog check. If the vehicle has an emissions problem, the right fix is to diagnose and repair it.
